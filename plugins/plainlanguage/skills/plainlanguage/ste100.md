# STE100: the controlled register

ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English (Issue 9, January 2025) is a controlled natural language: 53
writing rules plus a dictionary of approved words, each entry with one approved meaning. Aerospace
and defence maintenance documentation uses it so that a non-native reader can follow a procedure
without guessing.

This file compresses the rules and the parts of the dictionary that change general writing. The
standard itself is 434 pages and is not reproduced here: it is free from
<https://www.asd-ste100.org/STE_downloads.html> and its licence forbids redistribution. Cite rule
numbers when you apply one.

**What this file cannot do.** Full STE compliance is decided by the dictionary, entry by entry, not
by this summary. Text produced from this reference is STE-style, not certified STE. If a document
must be called STE-compliant, every word in it has to be checked against Part 2 of the standard. Say
which you are doing: writing to the register, or claiming the standard.

## When this register applies

Use it for text a stranger has to execute or obey without asking anyone:

- installation, maintenance, and operating procedures
- checklists and work instructions
- safety warnings, cautions, and notes
- runbooks, setup steps, and migration instructions
- reference documentation for a non-native audience

Do not use it for announcements, marketing, essays, chat replies, release-note commentary, or
anything where voice carries meaning. A controlled language is not a style improvement. Applied to
persuasive or narrative prose it reads as a translated manual.

Scope it per passage. A changelog with three migration steps in it gets the register on those three
steps and ordinary plain language everywhere else.

## How this file relates to the rest of the skill

The register adds constraints on top of [SKILL.md](SKILL.md) and also mandates specific word choices,
which is not the same thing as being a subset of it. Two consequences:

- Inside a controlled-register passage, this file wins where the two disagree. It will choose longer,
  plainer, more repetitive wording (*AT THIS TIME* over *now*, *MAKE SURE* over *ensure*) and it will
  flatten sentence rhythms that [SKILL.md](SKILL.md) would otherwise vary.
- Outside a controlled-register passage, nothing in this file applies. Do not carry the word swaps or
  the counts into ordinary prose.

One rule outranks every limit here: **never break grammar, drop a condition, or change an obligation
to make a count come out right.** Use a vertical list (4.3), split the step, or flag the conflict.

## The hard limits

| Limit | Value | Rule |
|-|-|-|
| Words in one instruction sentence | 20 | 5.1 |
| Words in one descriptive sentence | 25 | 6.3 |
| Words in a multi-word noun | 3 | 2.1 |
| Sentences in one paragraph | 6 | 6.6 |
| Instructions per sentence | 1 | 5.2 |
| Topics per paragraph | 1 | 6.5 |
| Semicolons per document | 0 | 8.1 |

Word counting has its own rules (8.4 to 8.7). Numbers, numbers with units, abbreviations,
alphanumeric identifiers, quoted text, titles and placards, and proper nouns each count as one word.
Hyphenated words count as one. In a vertical list, a colon counts like a full stop. Text in
parentheses counts as one word, but the text inside the parentheses is still written to the rules.

## Part 1: the 53 rules

### Section 1, words (1.1 to 1.14)

1.1 Use words that are approved in the dictionary, or are technical nouns, or are technical verbs.
1.2 Use an approved word only as the part of speech the dictionary gives it.
1.3 Use an approved word only with its approved meaning.
1.4 Use only the approved forms of verbs and adjectives.
1.5 A word outside the dictionary is admissible if it fits an approved technical noun category.
1.6 A non-approved word is admissible only when it is, or is part of, a technical noun.
1.7 Do not use a technical noun as a verb.
1.8 Use technical nouns that your company, industry, or subject field approves.
1.9 When you must coin a technical noun, keep it short (three words at most) and easy to understand.
1.10 Never use a regional, slang, or jargon word as a technical noun.
1.11 Do not use different technical nouns for the same item.
1.12 The same category test admits technical verbs.
1.13 Do not use a technical verb as a noun.
1.14 Use American English spelling unless an official directive says otherwise. **Not applied in this
repo**: pick one spelling convention and hold it for the whole document.

### Section 2, multi-word nouns (2.1 to 2.2)

2.1 Write multi-word nouns of no more than three words. To keep one short, restructure it with
prepositions (*the cover of the access panel*, not *the access panel cover assembly*).
2.2 When a technical noun has more than three words, write it in full, then either give a shorter form
or hyphenate the words that act as one unit. If it cannot be divided (a part number, a placarded
name, an approved component designation), write it as it is in its approved form. Never distort an
official name to satisfy the count.

### Section 3, verbs (3.1 to 3.7)

3.1 Use only the verb forms the dictionary gives.
3.2 The permitted forms and tenses are the infinitive, the imperative, the simple present, the simple
past, the simple future, and the past participle used as an adjective. No present perfect, no past
perfect, no progressive, no other compound construction.
3.3 Use the past participle as an adjective ("the adjusted linkage").
3.4 Do not build complex verb constructions with auxiliary verbs.
3.5 Use the "-ing" form only as a technical noun or as a modifier inside a technical noun.
3.6 Use the active voice. In descriptive writing, passive is permitted only when the agent is
unknown.
3.7 Describe an action with an approved verb, not with a noun or another part of speech.

### Section 4, sentences (4.1 to 4.5)

4.1 Write short, clear sentences.
4.2 Do not drop words or use contractions to shorten a sentence.
4.3 Use a vertical list for complex text. This is the escape when a sentence will not fit its limit.
4.4 Join sentences on related topics with a connecting word or phrase.
4.5 Put an article (*the*, *a*, *an*) or a demonstrative adjective (*this*, *these*) before a noun or
multi-word noun where one applies.

### Section 5, procedural writing (5.1 to 5.5)

5.1 Write sentences of 20 words or fewer.
5.2 One instruction per sentence. Two instructions may share a sentence only when the actions happen
at the same time. Splitting simultaneous actions into separate steps changes the procedure.
5.3 Write instructions in the imperative.
5.4 When the reader must know a condition first, open with the descriptive statement, then a comma,
then the command.
5.5 A note carries information only. It must not carry an instruction, a requirement, or a limit.

### Section 6, descriptive writing (6.1 to 6.6)

6.1 Give information gradually rather than in one dense block.
6.2 Use key words and key phrases to give the text a logical structure.
6.3 Write sentences of 25 words or fewer.
6.4 Use paragraphs to group related information.
6.5 One topic per paragraph.
6.6 Six sentences maximum per paragraph.

### Section 7, safety instructions (7.1 to 7.3)

7.1 Label the level of risk with the applicable word. **Warning** means risk of injury or death.
**Caution** means risk of damage to machines, tools, or equipment. When both risks are present, use a
warning.
7.2 Start the safety instruction with a clear, accurate command or condition. If the reader must know
something before the step begins, that condition comes first.
7.3 Explain the risk or the possible result.

Never soften or strengthen a safety label that came from the source. If the source does not say which
level applies, flag it rather than choosing.

### Section 8, punctuation and word count (8.1 to 8.7)

8.1 Every standard English punctuation mark is available except the semicolon.
8.2 Use a hyphen to connect words that are directly related.
8.3 Parentheses are for references to illustrations or text, item identifiers, work-step numbers,
explanations, alternatives, singular and plural forms, and abbreviations.
8.4 In a vertical list, a colon counts as a full stop for word count.
8.5 Text in parentheses counts as one word in that sentence.
8.6 Numbers, numbers with units, abbreviations, alphanumeric identifiers, quoted text, titles and
placards, and proper nouns each count as one word.
8.7 A hyphenated word counts as one word.

### Section 9, writing practices (9.1 to 9.4)

9.1 When a word-for-word replacement produces a bad sentence, rewrite the construction instead.
9.2 Use each approved word correctly, in its approved sense.
9.3 Do not coin a phrasal verb by putting two words together. The two-word verbs the dictionary lists
itself (*COME ON*, *GO OFF*, *PUT ON*, *MAKE SURE*) are approved; new ones are not.
9.4 Choose terminology once and keep it consistent.

## General recommendations (GR-1 to GR-8)

These are recommendations that sit beside the rules, not numbered rules. Use them as tie-breakers, not
as citations of equal weight.

- **GR-1** Keep the conjunction *that* where its removal makes the sentence ambiguous.
- **GR-2** Use *with* for "having" or "using", not as a loose connector.
- **GR-3** A pronoun needs an antecedent the reader can identify without guessing.
- **GR-4** *This* must point at one identifiable thing, not at a whole preceding argument.
- **GR-5** Watch false friends: words that look like a native-language word but mean something else.
- **GR-6** Avoid Latin abbreviations (*e.g.*, *i.e.*, *etc.*); write the English equivalent.
- **GR-7** Use gender-neutral language. Gendered pronouns are not permitted, and *man* or *woman*
  appears only where the context needs it, as in a medical text.
- **GR-8** The possessive form is permitted but optional. Drop it when you are unsure, because
  non-native readers often cannot parse it.

## Recurring errors

The words writers most often get wrong. Left column: rejected. Right column: what STE approves.
Uppercase means the word is approved in the dictionary; lowercase means it is not.

| Do not write | Write |
|-|-|
| acceptable | PERMITTED |
| alternate | ALTERNATIVE |
| avoid | PREVENT |
| both | THE TWO |
| complete (adj) | COMPLETED |
| ensure | MAKE SURE |
| fit | INSTALL |
| further | MORE |
| have to | imperative action verb |
| however | BUT |
| insert | PUT |
| main (adj) | PRIMARY |
| need, required | NECESSARY |
| now | AT THIS TIME |
| old | REMAINING, USED, or EXPIRED |
| people | PERSON or PERSONNEL |
| perform | DO |
| portion | PART |
| press | PUSH |
| reach | GET |
| repeat | DO ... AGAIN (or DO ... a second time) |
| rotate | TURN |
| secure | ATTACH or SAFETY |
| since | BECAUSE |
| therefore | THUS or AS A RESULT |
| under | BELOW, IN, or LESS THAN |
| using | USE or WITH |

Five rows need judgement, and three of them can change the meaning of a sentence if you apply them
mechanically:

- **may to CAN.** STE has no permission modal. If the source grants permission rather than ability,
  do not convert it to capability. Write *you are permitted to*, or keep the source wording and flag
  it.
- **should to MUST.** STE has no advisory modal either. Turning advice into a requirement is a
  semantic change, and in a regulation or a safety document it is a real one. Write *it is
  recommended that* using RECOMMEND, or flag the conflict.
- **over and under.** The replacement depends on the sense. Choose per sentence; never swap in bulk.
- **check, test, damage, cover.** These are approved as nouns, not as verbs. Do not emit a bare noun
  where a verb belongs. Use EXAMINE, MAKE SURE, or DO a check of, per the dictionary entry.
- **follow.** FOLLOW is approved for movement and sequence (*the cable follows the frame*). It is not
  approved for obedience, which is why OBEY appears above. Same rule, different sense, and the reason
  the verb list below is not a list of permissions.

## Where this file departs from the standard

- **Spelling (1.14).** Not applied. STE mandates American English. This repo does not. Hold one
  convention for the whole document and do not mix them.
- **Dictionary scope (1.1 to 1.13).** Only the recurring-error rows and the verb list are carried. The
  full 3,000-entry dictionary stays in the standard, which is why a compliance claim needs the standard
  itself.
- **Compliance claims.** This file can make text read like STE. It cannot certify it.

The semicolon ban (8.1) is not a departure: it is the standard, and it applies inside the register
only.

## How to check a passage

1. Count the words in each sentence. Over 20 in a procedure or 25 in description: split it, or make it
   a vertical list. Never delete a condition to hit the number.
2. Count the sentences in each paragraph. Over six: split the paragraph.
3. Count the words in each multi-word noun. Over three: restructure with a preposition, or shorten it,
   unless it is an official name.
4. Find the semicolons. Each one becomes two sentences or a list.
5. Find the contractions and the dropped articles. Put them back (4.2, 4.5).
6. Find every verb and check it against the list. Off-list is fine only for a declared technical verb.
7. Find every rejected word in the recurring-error table. Apply the five judgement rows by sense, not
   by swap.
8. Find the passive voice. Keep it only in description where the agent is unknown.
9. Find every safety label. Check it against the source: warning for injury or death, caution for
   equipment damage, note for information only.
